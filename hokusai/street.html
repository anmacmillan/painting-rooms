Under the Wave off Kanagawa

You steer a fast Edo rowing boat across the bay off Kanagawa — ride the swells and tuck low under the Great Wave to frame Mount Fuji in its hollow.

Katsushika Hokusai · Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, about 1830–32

Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave)

Katsushika Hokusai designed this woodblock print in Edo around 1830–32, when he was seventy years old, to open his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (which proved so popular he added ten more views). This impression hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York (JP1847).

Why Mount Fuji looks so small: Hokusai studied European perspective from Dutch prints imported through Nagasaki and dropped the viewer right down onto the water. Because you look up from a low boat, a storm wave close to the prow towers over Japan’s highest volcano far away on the horizon — and even the small foreground wave echoes Fuji’s snowy peak.

A storm wave, not a tsunami: The three narrow wooden boats (oshiokuri-bune) are fast Edo cargo and fishing boats being sculled across the bay. Oceanographers conclude the breaking crest is an unusually large open-sea storm or rogue wave rather than a tsunami, which stays low in deep water.

The new blue: The rich blues — including the print’s carved blue outlines — come from Prussian blue (“Berlin blue”), a synthetic dye invented in Germany that had just reached Japan in affordable quantities and resisted fading far better than older blues. The layered open bay and voyage are the game’s own; the Great Wave, boats and Fuji come straight from Hokusai’s print.
