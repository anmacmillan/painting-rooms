Under the Wave off Kanagawa

The sea off Kanagawa is rising — ride the swells toward Mount Fuji and let the Great Wave carry you home.

Katsushika Hokusai · Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, about 1830–32

Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave)

Katsushika Hokusai designed this woodblock print in Edo around 1830–32, when he was seventy years old, to open his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (which proved so popular he added ten more views). This impression hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York (JP1847).

Why Mount Fuji looks so small: Hokusai studied European perspective from Dutch prints imported through Nagasaki and dropped the viewer right down onto the water. In every trough, a swell close to the boat hides Japan’s highest volcano on the far horizon — and on every crest Fuji reappears, small and still, echoed by the snowy peak of the foreground wave.

A storm wave, not a tsunami: The three narrow wooden boats (oshiokuri-bune) are fast Edo cargo and fishing boats crossing Edo Bay. Oceanographers conclude the breaking crest is an unusually large open-sea storm or rogue wave rather than a tsunami, which stays low in deep water.

The new blue: The rich blues — including the print’s carved blue outlines — come from Prussian blue (“Berlin blue”), a synthetic dye invented in Germany that had just reached Japan in affordable quantities and resisted fading far better than older blues. The layered open bay ride is the game’s own; the Great Wave, boats and Fuji at the end come straight from Hokusai’s print.
