## Verified Facts & Sources

1. **Title, series, publisher & date:**
   - Title in cartouche: *Fugaku sanjūrokkei / Kanagawa-oki nami-ura* (*Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji / Under the Wave off Kanagawa*).
   - Date: **ca. 1830–32** (Met API `objectID 45434` and `39799`: `ca. 1830–32`; V&A API `O73124`: `ca. 1831`, `O407314`: `ca. 1830–31`; Art Institute of Chicago API `id 24645`: `1830/33`, noting the series was begun in 1830 when Hokusai was 70 years old).
   - Publisher: Nishimura-ya Yohachi (Art Institute of Chicago `id 24645`).
   - Series size: *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* was the first print series devoted exclusively to large-format (*ōban*) landscapes (V&A `O73124`), and proved so popular that Hokusai added 10 more views, making 46 prints in total (Art Institute of Chicago `id 87008`).
   - Popularity & price: Roughly 5,000–8,000 impressions were originally printed; in 1842 the official price of a single sheet was fixed at 16 *mon* (about a double helping of noodles) (BBC / British Museum, *A History of the World in 100 Objects*, Episode 93, `https://www.bbc.co.uk/ahistoryoftheworld/about/transcripts/episode93/`).

2. **Prussian Blue ("Berlin Blue") & Blue Key Block:**
   - The series' striking blue is **Prussian blue** (also called Berlin blue), a synthetic pigment invented in Germany in the early 18th century that resisted fading far better than traditional vegetable blues (BBC / British Museum Ep. 93; V&A `O73124`; Art Institute of Chicago `id 24645`).
   - It reached Japan through Nagasaki trade (via Dutch or Chinese merchants) and became newly affordable around 1829–30, just as Hokusai launched the Fuji series (BBC / British Museum Ep. 93; AIC `id 24645`).
   - Crucially, the Rijksmuseum records of *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (`RP-P-1956-733`, `https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/RP-P-1956-733`) note `lijnblok in blauw met kleurblokken` — the key-block outlines themselves were printed in blue, alongside graded colour blocks.

3. **Mount Fuji, Low Horizon & Perspective (The Core Teaching Point):**
   - Snow-capped Mount Fuji — Japan's highest mountain — sits tiny in the far distance, framed through the hollow of the towering foreground wave, contrasting the restless chaos of the sea with the serene stillness of the peak (V&A `O73124`, `O422752`; AIC `id 24645`).
   - Hokusai studied European perspective from Dutch prints imported through Nagasaki and used it "to push Mount Fuji far into the distance", leaving the viewer with "no footing: you too must be in a boat, under 'The Great Wave'" (BBC / British Museum Ep. 93).

4. **The Boats & Crew:**
   - The print shows **three narrow wooden rowing/cargo boats** (*oshiokuri-bune*) being sculled across Edo (Tokyo) Bay against the wave's direction of travel, with crewmen crouched low inside the hulls (Dudley, Sarano & Dias 2013, *Notes and Records of the Royal Society* 67(2):159–164, `PMC3645210`; Ornes 2014, *PNAS* 111(37):13245, `PMC4169912`; Rijksmuseum `RP-P-1956-733`; BBC / British Museum Ep. 93).

5. **Storm / Rogue Wave — NOT a Tsunami:**
   - Although frequently mislabelled a tsunami in popular culture, oceanographers Cartwright & Nakamura (2009, *Notes and Records of the Royal Society* 63(2):119–135, DOI `10.1098/rsnr.2007.0039`, abstract verified via Crossref API) and Dudley, Sarano & Dias (2013, `PMC3645210`) conclude that Hokusai depicted a steep plunging storm/rogue wave (~10 m tall) on open water (~3 km offshore in Tokyo Bay), not a tsunami (which travels at low amplitude on open water and only rears near shore).
   - Dudley et al. (2013) further note that because a rogue wave is localized transversely (such as from directional focusing of crossing wave trains), skilled boat crews could navigate around or through its swell without being overwhelmed.
