# Research Findings: Utagawa Kuniyoshi — *Takiyasha the Witch and the Skeleton Spectre*

All facts used in `kuniyoshi/street.html` are verified below. Anything not confirmed from a primary museum or library source is marked clearly.

## 1. The Print

- **Title (British Museum / V&A English title):** *Takiyasha the Witch and the Skeleton Spectre* (Commons file description: *"Colour woodblock print, triptych. Princess Takiyasha summoning a skeleton spectre to frighten Mitsukuni at her father Taira Masakado's ruined palace at Soma. Signed, sealed, marked and inscribed."*)
  - **Sources:**
    - Wikimedia Commons (`https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Takiyasha_the_Witch_and_the_Skeleton_Spectre,_by_Utagawa_Kuniyoshi.jpg`), reproducing British Museum catalogue record `1915,0823,0.915-916` (object URL `https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1915-0823-0-915-916`, HTTP 403 to direct automated fetch; text quoted on Commons citing *Smith et al 1990*).
    - Victoria & Albert Museum (`https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/japanese-woodblock-prints-ukiyo-e`), illustrating another impression of the same triptych: *Takiyasha the Witch and the Skeleton Spectre*, woodblock print, Utagawa Kuniyoshi, c. 1844, Japan, Museum no. E1333:1 to 3-1922.
- **Artist:** Utagawa Kuniyoshi (1797–1861; note Wikipedia gives 1797–1861 in the caption and 1798–1861 in the opening sentence — we do not print birth year unless needed, or note "c. 1844").
- **Date:** circa 1844 (confirmed by both British Museum / Commons record and V&A article).
- **Format and size:** Colour woodblock triptych on paper; three sheets, each 37 cm high × 25 cm wide (Commons / British Museum record `1915,0823,0.915-916`).
- **Where it hangs / collection:** This impression (`1915,0823,0.915-916`) is in the British Museum, London (Asia collection). Another impression (`E1333:1 to 3-1922`) is in the Victoria and Albert Museum, London.
- **Public domain status:** Public domain artwork (Kuniyoshi died 1861); Wikimedia Commons file `File:Takiyasha the Witch and the Skeleton Spectre, by Utagawa Kuniyoshi.jpg` marked PD-Art. Local file `kuniyoshi/images/takiyasha.jpg` (3840 × 1910 px) is the sole external artwork used in this level.

## 2. Subject & Story (from British Museum note reproduced on Commons, Smith et al. 1990)

- **Historical background:** Until his defeat and destruction in AD 939, the provincial warlord Taira no Masakado set up a rival "Eastern Court" in Shimōsa Province, in rebellion against the court in Kyoto.
- **Setting:** Masakado's ruined palace at Sōma, inhabited after his death by his daughter Princess Takiyasha (shown on the left sheet), a sorceress.
- **Action:** Takiyasha reads an incantation from a scroll to summon a giant skeleton spectre, which rips away the tattered palace blinds with its bony fingers to menace the warrior Ōya no Tarō Mitsukuni (kneeling, centre-left), sent by the imperial court to investigate surviving conspirators.
- **Anatomical source:** The British Museum note observes that Kuniyoshi is said to have owned imported Western prints and likely drew on a Western medical illustration for the anatomically detailed skeleton.
- **Historical context (Tenpō Reforms):** The story had been staged as a Kabuki play in 1836 and again in 1844; the British Museum note records the suggestion that Kuniyoshi framed the scene historically because the Tenpō Reforms had temporarily banned direct depictions of Kabuki actors and theatrical scenes in colour woodblock prints.
- **Note on the second man (centre-right):** The red cartouche beside the kneeling figure on the right of the centre sheet reads 荒井丸 (Arai-maru) on the print itself, and Wikipedia refers only to "Mitsukuni and his companion". Because the British Museum catalogue note quoted on Commons does not name the second man, our game text refers to him simply as Mitsukuni's companion rather than asserting an unverified biographical detail.

## 3. Japanese Colour Woodblock Printing (*Ukiyo-e* / *Nishiki-e*)

Verified directly from two museum/national-library sources:

1. **Victoria and Albert Museum — *Japanese woodblock prints (ukiyo-e)*** (`https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/japanese-woodblock-prints-ukiyo-e`):
   - Multi-colour woodblock printing was perfected in **1765**; the resulting full-colour prints were called ***nishiki-e*** ("brocade pictures").
   - Production was a collaboration of four specialists (the *ukiyo-e* quartet): **publisher**, **designer/artist**, **block cutter**, and **printer**.
   - **Key-block:** The artist drew the design on paper; an exact copy was pasted face-down onto a **cherry wood** block (chosen for its fine, even grain and consistent density), and the block cutter carved directly through the copy to create the black outline **key-block**.
   - **Registration and colour blocks:** The printer pulled black-and-white **key-block proofs** carrying **registration marks** (small carved stops outside the picture area). Separate woodblocks were then carved **one for each colour** needed in the final print, using those registration marks so the printer could line up every colour precisely in register over the black outline.
2. **Library of Congress — *The Floating World of Ukiyo-e*** (`https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/ukiyo-e/intro.html`):
   - Confirms the four-person team (publisher, artist, carver, printer), printing on **handmade paper**, and notes that during the Edo period a colour print typically used **ten to sixteen** woodblocks.
3. **Secondary (Wikipedia — *Woodblock printing in Japan*, `https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodblock_printing_in_Japan`):**
   - Gives the Japanese terms ***kentō*** (見当, the L-shaped and straight registration stops carved in the margin of each block), ***baren*** (hand-held bamboo-wrapped rubbing pad used to press paper onto the inked block), ***sumizuri-e*** (monochrome black-ink print), and ***washi*** (Japanese paper), plus notes water-based inks (unlike Western oil-based woodcut). Where used in our UI/end card, plain English explanations ("registration marks", "hand-rubbed pad", "key block") accompany any Japanese term so no claim depends on Wikipedia alone.

## 4. What Could Not Be Verified Directly

- Direct automated fetches to `britishmuseum.org` (HTTP 403) and `metmuseum.org` (HTTP 429) were blocked; British Museum facts therefore come via the verbatim catalogue transcription on Wikimedia Commons.
- Exact number of physical woodblocks used to print this specific Kuniyoshi triptych (`1915,0823,0.915-916`) is not recorded in the sources retrieved. Our game separates the print's visible palette into **five colour blocks** (straw & ochre, blue, red, smoke grey, and night black) printed over the black **key block** to demonstrate the real multi-block registration process clearly within 2–3 minutes of play; the end card states plainly that the real Edo print used more blocks (typically ten to sixteen, per Library of Congress) and that our five-block sequence is a simplification for the game.
- Whether Kuniyoshi himself used Prussian blue (*bero-ai*) on this specific impression was not confirmed by pigment analysis in the retrieved museum pages; we therefore call the blue block simply "Indigo & Blue" rather than claiming Prussian blue chemistry.
